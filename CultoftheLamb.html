REVIEWS / ACTION ROGUELITE · 2022

Cult of the Lamb
Style Over Substance?

Cult of the Lamb combines dungeon crawling, town management, and an unmistakably striking art style. There's a good game beneath all that sinister imagery, but its reliance on shock value and repetitive systems makes this a more complicated recommendation than its adorable cast might suggest.

REVIEW BY DARK RAVEN PLAYED ON PC ~ 16 HOURS

Cult of the Lamb is the sort of game that makes a strong first impression. Its colorful characters, bold presentation, and unusual premise immediately stand out in a crowded indie market. Unfortunately, the more time I spent with it, the more I found myself wondering how much of its identity comes from genuinely engaging ideas and how much comes from simply trying to shock its audience. There is a worthwhile game here, but not every provocative choice adds much to the experience.

The short version

Cult of the Lamb is an action roguelite built around a settlement-management game. You play a resurrected lamb bound to a mysterious supernatural entity, tasked with building a following and defeating rival bishops. Between dungeon expeditions, you return to camp to gather resources, construct buildings, feed your followers, and perform rituals to keep your growing congregation in line.

The combination is a clever idea. Dungeon runs supply the materials needed to improve your settlement, while those improvements help you prepare for more dangerous expeditions. There's a natural rhythm to the back-and-forth, and unlocking new buildings and abilities gives you a steady stream of short-term goals. For a while, the two halves complement each other nicely.

The problem is that neither side develops quite as deeply as the premise suggests it might. Once the initial novelty wears off, the routine becomes familiar: venture out, collect what you need, return home, maintain the camp, and repeat.

What stands out

The presentation is undoubtedly the game's strongest asset. Its expressive animal characters, distinctive animation, and colorful environments give it an identity you can recognize immediately. The contrast between its cute visual style and its grim subject matter is clearly intentional, and the developers have committed to that aesthetic with considerable confidence.

Combat is enjoyable in short bursts. Weapons have different feels, dodge timing matters, and tarot-card bonuses provide some welcome variation between runs. Boss encounters offer a few satisfying challenges, and the controls are generally responsive. The settlement side also has some appeal: arranging buildings, unlocking facilities, and watching your camp expand can be rewarding when you're working toward a particular goal.

Most importantly, the game deserves credit for attempting something a little different. Combining an action roguelite with a management simulator creates a clear gameplay loop, and the art direction helps the experience stand apart from more conventional fantasy adventures. There is genuine creativity here, even when the execution doesn't always live up to the potential.

Where it stumbles

Repetition is the biggest mechanical weakness. Dungeon encounters begin to feel interchangeable, and the relatively straightforward combat doesn't always provide enough depth to sustain long stretches of play. Camp management can also turn into a list of chores, with resource gathering and follower maintenance interrupting the parts of the game that are actually moving things forward.

The writing and world-building are another mixed bag. The premise has plenty of room for meaningful exploration of faith, authority, loyalty, and manipulation, but the game often seems more interested in the immediate reaction its imagery can provoke. Rituals, sacrifice, and deliberately grotesque humor are used as prominent parts of its identity. For some players, that irreverence will be the main attraction. For me, it too often feels like provocation for its own sake.

Dark subject matter isn't inherently a problem, and games should be free to explore uncomfortable ideas. The question is whether those choices contribute something worthwhile beyond the shock itself. Here, I wasn't always convinced. The contrast between cute animals and disturbing rituals can be memorable, but it doesn't automatically create meaningful characters or a deeply immersive world.

That distinction matters when comparing it with a game like Stardew Valley. Stardew's appeal comes from the care invested in its setting, the personal stories of its residents, and the little details that make its world feel alive. Cult of the Lamb has a strong visual identity, but its characters and systems don't consistently inspire that same sense of attachment.

Final thoughts

Cult of the Lamb is an inventive indie game with a memorable presentation and a gameplay concept that works well enough to carry a substantial adventure. Its combat is entertaining, its settlement systems provide a satisfying sense of progression, and its art direction is difficult to mistake for anything else. Those strengths deserve recognition.

Personally, though, I came away more conflicted than impressed. I appreciate the imagination behind the project, but the emphasis on disturbing imagery and religious provocation often felt more interested in generating a reaction than building a world I wanted to inhabit. I don't need every game to be wholesome, and I certainly don't expect every story to share my values. Still, I appreciate games that earn my investment through depth, thoughtful writing, and characters who feel like more than pieces in a clever design.

Cult of the Lamb has enough good ideas to be worth playing, but its repetitive gameplay and reliance on shock value hold it back from being something truly special. It's a distinctive and occasionally enjoyable experience, just not one whose darker sensibilities worked for me. A 7.6 feels fair: credit for the creativity, with reservations about what lies beneath the surface.
